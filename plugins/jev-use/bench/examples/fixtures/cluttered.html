Coffee rust is climbing into the cool slopes that used to be safe

By Ruth Oyelaran, Climate desk · Published 14 March, 06:12 · Updated 09:40 · 7 min read
SharePostSendSaveGift this article 1.2k shares

For thirty years the advice given to coffee growers across the highlands was simple and it worked: plant higher. Leaf rust, the orange fungus that strips a coffee tree of the leaves it needs to ripen a crop, faded out somewhere above 1,500 metres, where the nights ran cold enough to stall it. This season the advice failed. Surveyors working three separate mountain ranges found established rust on terraces at 1,780 and 1,830 metres, on farms that had never reported it.

The pattern is not a single outbreak but a slow arrival. Growers in four cooperatives described the same sequence over two seasons: a few spotted leaves in the wettest corner of a plot, no visible loss at harvest, then a second year in which whole rows turned and the cherries stayed small and hollow. Because the first year costs almost nothing, it is routinely missed. By the time the loss shows up in the weight at the collection point, the spores have had eighteen months to settle in.

Two things changed at once, and untangling them is the current argument. Minimum night temperatures on the upper terraces have risen by roughly 1.1 degrees over two decades, which lengthens the window in which spores can germinate. At the same time the varieties planted through the last replanting drive, chosen for cup quality and yield, carry markedly less resistance than the rough older stock they replaced. Neither factor alone explains terraces at 1,830 metres. Together they do.

A warning system that arrives after the loss

Most growing regions still detect rust the way they did in the 1990s: an agronomist walks the rows, or a farmer calls someone. Trials of a cheaper method — photographing marked leaves on a fixed schedule and scoring the images — cut the average time between first infection and first treatment from eleven weeks to under three in the plots where it was tried. The obstacle is not the camera. It is that the scoring has to happen every week on thousands of images, and nobody is paid to look at them.

We are not short of information. We are short of anyone looking at the information in the week it matters. Marta Quiñones, agronomist, eleven seasons surveying the northern ranges

The economics are unforgiving in a specific way. A fungicide round costs a smallholder roughly 9 per cent of the season's gross if it is applied on time and does its job. Applied a month late, it costs the same and recovers perhaps a third of the yield. The decision, in other words, is worth almost nothing on the day it is cheap and almost everything a few weeks later, which is precisely the shape of decision that gets deferred.

What the cooperatives are doing instead

Three of the four cooperatives have stopped waiting for a regional forecast and started their own thresholds, tied to nothing more sophisticated than a cheap logger on a post and a rule about consecutive humid nights. It is coarse, and it produces false alarms, but a false alarm costs one wasted round of spraying and a missed infection costs a third of a year's income. Two cooperatives have also quietly gone back to interplanting the older, uglier, more resistant stock along the wet edges of their plots.

None of this is a solution at the scale of the problem, and everyone interviewed said so. The resistant varieties that breeders are working toward are eight to twelve years from the terraces even on optimistic timelines. What the last two seasons changed is narrower and more useful: the altitude line that growers, lenders and buyers all quietly relied on is no longer where anyone thought it was, and the people who planted above it did so on a promise that has expired.
